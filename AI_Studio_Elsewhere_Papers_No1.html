AI Studio Elsewhere Papers · No. 1

Cinematic Localization Engine

A Human-in-the-Loop Agent Architecture
for Narrative Film Translation

Luca D'Isanto
AI Studio Elsewhere

Every film has a voice.

It lives somewhere between language and silence.

Traditional subtitle systems translate words.

Films ask us to translate intention.

This document describes the architecture of a localization system designed around that difference. It is written for filmmakers, translators, festival programmers, and anyone who has ever watched a film in a language they do not speak and understood every word.

Chapter One

Why Translation Is Not Localization

In 1962, Michelangelo Antonioni released L'Eclisse. The film ends with eight minutes of footage in which neither of the two main characters appears. Streets, a streetlamp, a bus stop, water, bark. Silence. The last shot fades to black. There is no dialogue to translate. And yet that ending communicates something no subtitle can transcribe.

Film is a medium in which language is never the whole message. A line of dialogue carries meaning in at least four directions simultaneously: what the words say, what they conceal, how they are delivered, and what precedes and follows them in the scene. Translation addresses the first. Localization must address all four.

Consider a simple example. A character says: 可我覺得賤。 Word for word, this is: But I feel cheap. A translation tool returns: But I feel cheap. It is not wrong. It is also not the line.

The character speaking is a woman in the middle of a scene of bitter self-awareness. She has just described something she did. She is not asking for sympathy. She is stating a verdict about herself with the precision of someone who has thought about it for a long time. The German word schäbig — worn, shabby, mean — carries a weight that billig (cheap) or wertlos (worthless) do not. It sounds like what it means. It feels like judgment.

That difference is not linguistic. It is directorial.

Dialogue in film is not speech. It is constructed sound. A screenwriter chooses each word for its rhythm as much as its meaning. I feel schäbig has a different weight in the body than I feel worthless. Subtitle readers hear the original language and read the translation simultaneously, and the two must align at the level of feeling — not vocabulary.

Then there is silence. In Wong Kar-wai's cinema, what characters do not say defines the scene as much as what they speak aloud. A character who asks a question and receives no answer communicates something that a translated non-answer must preserve — the rhythm of the refusal, the length of the pause, the word that is chosen to fill the space. There is no dictionary entry for that.

And there are recurring motifs. A phrase used once early in a film and again near the end is not just repeated — it is transformed. The second time, every connotation of the first usage accompanies it. If the translation drifts between the two occurrences, the motif dissolves. The audience feels a faint loss and cannot say why.

Translation converts language.
Localization preserves voice.

The distinction is not philosophical. It is practical. It determines every decision made downstream — about which word to choose, which rhythm to maintain, which phrase to lock in place across the entire film. A localization system built on translation assumptions will produce correct output. It will not produce the right output.

Chapter Two

The Director Remains the Author

Before describing how the system works, it is worth describing what the system does not do.

The Cinematic Localization Engine does not decide the tone of the film. It does not choose which words are important. It does not determine how a character should sound. It does not have opinions about the relationship between silence and text.

The director does.

This is not a technical constraint. It is a philosophical commitment. Localization is an act of authorship. When a film crosses a language boundary, something is inevitably lost in the crossing. The question is not whether to lose something — that is unavoidable — but what to preserve at the cost of everything else. That decision belongs to the person who made the film.

The system's role is to execute that decision consistently, at scale, without drift, and across every line of the film. That is not a small contribution. A feature film can contain twelve hundred subtitle events. Maintaining tonal coherence across twelve hundred decisions — each made at a different moment, often by different people, always under time pressure — is precisely where human localization work has historically failed. This is where a well-designed system helps.

The system never decides the artistic direction.
The director writes the Bible.
The system executes it faithfully.

This distinction — between deciding and executing — is the architectural principle that determines every design choice in the system. The director's voice is not an input to the process. It is the process. Everything else is infrastructure built to serve it.

In practice, this means the director interacts with the system before any translation begins, establishing the rules that will govern every subsequent decision. And it means the director interacts again after the system has run, reviewing what was produced and editing what does not serve the film.

Between those two moments, the system works alone. But it works in the director's service, not in its own.

Chapter Three

The Localization Bible

Every film production keeps a continuity bible. It is the document that the script supervisor maintains — the record of every detail that must remain consistent across the shoot. Which hand the character uses to hold the glass. Whether the window is open or closed. The color of the coat she is wearing in a scene that will cut to a scene shot three weeks later.

The Localization Bible is the same document, written for language.

It remembers how people speak. A character who uses short sentences, declarative and unadorned, should not suddenly develop subordinate clauses in the German version. A character whose speech is layered and elliptical should arrive in the target language with the same quality of indirection.

It remembers what they never say. In localization, omission is as important as inclusion. Some phrases are deliberately avoided for a character — not because they would be incorrect, but because they would be wrong for this person. The Bible holds those decisions.

It remembers recurring phrases. When a phrase appears more than once in a film, its translation is locked. The second occurrence carries the memory of the first. Drift at the second occurrence breaks the resonance the film has built.

And it carries the director's notes — the instructions that fall outside any category: this film is for Berlin, not streaming; the register is literary, not colloquial; this character never uses rhetorical questions.

The Localization Bible is the film's memory.
It does not change during production.
It may be revised between projects.

One rule governs the Bible absolutely: it is written before a batch of subtitles is processed, and it is frozen during processing. The system reads from it but never writes to it. A director may revise it between batches — after reviewing what the system produced, after identifying a drift that needs correcting. But while the system is running, the Bible does not move.

This constraint exists for the same reason that film continuity exists: the audience experiences a film as a single coherent work, even though it is assembled from fragments shot over months. The illusion of continuity requires that the rules remain stable across all the fragments.

At the technical level, the Bible is a structured document associated with a project. For a film called Bad Bird, it might contain the following:

Localization Bible — excerpt
Project: Bad Bird
Languages: Mandarin → German
Register: literary, restrained
Festival profile: Berlin IFF

Phrase locks:
  另一場生命 → ein anderes Leben

Character voices:
  Hong: provocative, sharp, self-destructive
  Qing: restrained, introspective

Director notes:
  Avoid rhetorical questions for Hong
  Prefer short lines over grammatical completeness

This is not configuration. It is craft documentation. The difference is important: configuration describes a system's behavior, while craft documentation describes a film's character. The Bible is the latter. The system reads it the way a performer reads a screenplay: as instruction about what kind of work is being made.

Chapter Four

Narrative Context

A subtitle does not exist in isolation. It is part of a moment, which is part of a scene, which is part of a film.

This seems obvious. In practice, most translation systems treat each subtitle line as an independent unit — a string of source text that produces a string of target text. The result is technically accurate and narratively incoherent. Individual lines pass quality checks while the scene loses its shape.

The Cinematic Localization Engine works from three concentric circles of context.

Narrative Context — three circles
Film
    ↓
Scene
    ↓
Moment

The Film. Every translation decision is made within the awareness of the whole film. What genre is this work? What register does it occupy in the landscape of cinema — arthouse, festival, commercial? What is its relationship to language — does it treat dialogue as music, as information, as percussion? Is it being prepared for a festival submission, where the economics of reading time are different from streaming? These questions shape the translation before a single line is processed.

The Scene. Each batch of subtitles belongs to a specific narrative moment. Who is in the scene? What is the relationship between them — and has that relationship changed since the last scene? What is the emotional purpose of this sequence: confrontation, revelation, transition, silence after violence? What came immediately before, and what comes immediately after? A scene that follows a rupture should sound different from a scene that precedes one, even if the dialogue itself seems neutral.

The Moment. At the level of the individual line: who is speaking? What is their emotional state in this instant — not in general, but now, during this sentence? What are they physically doing while they say it? A line delivered while walking away is not the same line delivered while standing still.

Context is not metadata.
It is direction.

Each of these three circles feeds a different part of the system. The film context shapes the register and style of translation. The scene context shapes the selection of tone from among available options. The moment context shapes both — it is the most granular and the most perishable.

The system never collapses these three circles into a single undifferentiated input. A line of dialogue that belongs to the film Bad Bird is influenced by all three levels simultaneously, but each level does a specific job — and collapsing them would prevent the system from knowing which level is responsible for a given decision.

Chapter Five

The Six Voices

Film is made by groups of people with distinct responsibilities. The director does not operate the camera. The cinematographer does not cut the edit. The script supervisor does not write the score. Each role exists because the work requires a kind of sustained attention that cannot be distributed — someone has to be responsible for this specific thing and nothing else.

The Cinematic Localization Engine is organized on the same principle. Six agents, each with a single responsibility, each accountable for a specific quality of the output.

Agent Film Analogy Responsibility
Segmentation Script Supervisor Ensures every line exists, has a timestamp, and belongs where it belongs. Flags anomalies. Never rewrites.
Translation Translator Produces three candidate versions of each line — not one. Offers options, not decisions.
Tone Calibration Dialogue Director Reads the candidates. Knows the character. Selects the line that serves the scene. This is the artistic core of the system.
Consistency Continuity Supervisor Applies the Bible. Ensures the phrase that appeared in reel one appears identically in reel three.
QA Director Supervising Editor Checks that the line is readable, displayable, and still serves the scene after all previous stages have touched it.
Output Post-production Delivers in every required format — timed SRT, bilingual master, festival version.

The Dialogue Director deserves particular attention, because it is where the system diverges most sharply from existing tools.

Most translation systems produce a single output per input. This system produces three. The translation agent does not know which one will be used. The dialogue director does. And the dialogue director makes its selection not on the basis of linguistic accuracy — that is the translator's job — but on the basis of character, scene, and dramatic context.

The dialogue director is informed, before it selects, of the inferred tone of the segment. It is shown a score for how well each candidate aligns with the character's established voice. It receives the scene context. Only then does it choose — and it must explain, in a single sentence, why.

The translator produces options.
The dialogue director produces a decision.

The continuity supervisor, operating after the dialogue director, holds a different kind of authority. It does not improve the line. It does not make it more cinematic. It asks one question only: does this line comply with what the Bible says? If it does, it passes unchanged. If it does not, it is adjusted — not reimagined, adjusted. The continuity supervisor is the least creative agent in the system and the most important for the long-term coherence of the work.

Chapter Six

The Conversation

For a single subtitle event — five words, four seconds — the system conducts the following conversation.

Processing a single line
Source line arrives
Script Supervisor: structure confirmed, timestamp verified
    ↓
Translator: three candidates produced
    ↓
Dialogue Director: tone inferred, candidates scored,
                 one line selected with rationale
    ↓
Continuity Supervisor: Bible consulted, locks applied
    ↓
Supervising Editor: length, reading speed, tone — approved
    ↓
Output: SRT block written

In the specific case of the line 可我覺得賤 from Bad Bird, the conversation went as follows.

The translator offered:

Aber ich fühle mich billig.
Aber ich fühle mich wertlos.
Aber ich fühle mich schäbig.

Three lines, each defensible. The dialogue director, knowing that the speaker is Hong — sharp, self-destructive, delivering a verdict rather than a complaint — and knowing that the scene is raw and self-aware, selected the third. Its rationale: stronger emotional nuance than billig; schäbig sounds like judgment, not victimhood.

The continuity supervisor found no phrase locks relevant to this line and passed it unchanged.

The supervising editor confirmed that Aber ich fühle mich schäbig fits within line-length constraints, reads at a comfortable pace within the allotted duration, and preserves the tone established by the dialogue director. Approved.

Five words. Four seconds. Six agents. One decision. That decision, made consistently across twelve hundred events, is what constitutes the voice of the film in another language.

The system also carries awareness of the lines surrounding each segment — the two lines before and the two lines after. This context window prevents a common failure mode in automated translation: the locally coherent, globally incoherent sequence. A line that reads perfectly in isolation can create a jarring discontinuity when placed between the lines that surround it in the cut.

Chapter Seven

Measuring Consistency Without Measuring Creativity

How do you know if a localization system is getting better?

The question sounds obvious. It is not. The difficulty is that the most important qualities of subtitle work — tone, character voice, dramatic coherence — resist quantification. A system that becomes more literally accurate may simultaneously become less cinematically alive. A system that improves its subtitle formatting may begin to lose the character of the speaker. These regressions are invisible if quality is measured as a single aggregate score.

The Cinematic Localization Engine addresses this by measuring quality across six independent dimensions, never combined into a single number.

Dimension What it measures
Meaning preservation Whether the source meaning and all its implications survive the translation.
Cinematic naturalness Whether the line sounds like spoken dialogue rather than translated text.
Tone alignment Whether the output matches the direction provided — scene, character, and segment.
Character voice Whether recurring speakers remain recognizably themselves across the film.
Subtitle fitness Length, line breaks, timing, and reading speed within display constraints.
Context use Whether the supplied context visibly influenced the output in the expected direction.

These dimensions are evaluated by a separate assessor — an evaluator that reviews source, reference, context, and output simultaneously, but never sees which pipeline version produced the output. This removes the possibility of systematic bias toward or against a particular approach.

The evaluation architecture also includes two categories of gates. Hard gates block a change from proceeding: missing phrase locks, invalid subtitle formatting, missing timestamps, severe meaning errors. These are non-negotiable.

Soft gates warn without blocking: a material decline in tone score, a drift in character consistency, a shift in human preference toward the previous version. Soft gate failures are evidence, not verdicts. They ask the director to look again before deciding.

A pipeline change could improve literal accuracy
while damaging dramatic tone.
The evaluation framework exposes that trade-off
rather than hiding it inside an average.

The system also includes a human preference layer — a simple pairwise comparison in which a director is shown the reference translation alongside the new output and asked which they would use. No numerical score. No forced ranking. Just: reference, candidate, no preference, unsure.

This is the most honest measurement available for artistic work. It does not ask a director to invent a number. It asks them to make the same kind of choice they make in the editing room, in the color suite, in the sound mix: this one or that one?

Over time, the accumulation of those choices becomes a dataset — a record of what this director, working on this film, consistently prefers. That record is more valuable than any automated score.

Chapter Eight

Human in the Loop

This phrase has become common in AI writing. It often means something modest: a human can review the output before it is published. The human is present at the end, as a check.

In the Cinematic Localization Engine, the human is present at every point that matters.

Before processing begins, the director writes the Bible. This is not optional configuration. It is the act that defines what the system is allowed to do. A system without a Bible has no artistic direction. It produces text. A system with a good Bible produces a version of the film in another language.

Before each batch, the director sets the context. What scene is this? What is its purpose? What came before? What follows? This act of framing — five minutes of attention before a hundred lines are processed — is worth more than any amount of post-processing correction. Direction given before the work begins propagates through every decision. Direction given after is repair.

After processing, the director reviews. Not every line — the system is designed so that most lines require no intervention. But the lines that are flagged, the lines where the system's confidence is low, the lines where tone and meaning pulled in different directions: these arrive already marked. The director does not search for problems. The system surfaces them.

AI performs localization.
Humans preserve authorship.

This distribution of labor is not a compromise. It is a design. The system does what scales poorly for humans: maintaining perfect consistency across twelve hundred decisions, applying every phrase lock without exception, checking every line against reading speed constraints. Humans do what scales poorly for systems: deciding what the film is about, holding the character's voice in mind, feeling when a line that passes every technical check is somehow still wrong.

There is a word in cinema for the person who maintains this kind of sustained artistic authority over a production while delegating execution to skilled collaborators.

Director.

The Cinematic Localization Engine is designed for directors. Not as a tool that makes localization easier. As an infrastructure that makes localization possible at the level of quality that the film deserves.

The complete pipeline
Director writes the Bible
    ↓
Director sets narrative context
    ↓
Six agents process the batch
    ↓
Director reviews flagged lines
    ↓
Evaluation harness measures quality
    ↓
Director approves and exports
    ↓
Festival delivery

Good subtitles disappear.

The audience does not remember the words. They remember the scene. They remember the feeling that moved through them when the character spoke — the weight of it, the precision, the silence that followed. They do not think: that was a good translation. They do not think about translation at all.

That invisibility is the measure of the work.

The Cinematic Localization Engine exists to serve that disappearance. Not to make AI visible in the work. Not to demonstrate what a system can do. To give the film the best possible chance of remaining itself when it crosses the boundary of language — recognizable, coherent, alive in the way it was alive in the original.

The purpose of the Cinematic Localization Engine
is not to make translation visible.
It is to let the film remain visible.
